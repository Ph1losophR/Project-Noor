# Deep Research: Why CDS Engines Fail

> Researched 2026-08-27 via firecrawl-deep-research (depth: thorough).
> ~26 primary sources saved and read. Claims are tagged inline with source URLs.
> Companion report: `how_cds_engines_work.md` (architecture and trends).

## Executive Summary

The evidence base converges on an uncomfortable conclusion: CDS engines rarely fail because their clinical knowledge is wrong — they fail because of **where, how, and how long** that knowledge is delivered. The strongest single datum is Kawamoto et al.'s BMJ meta-analysis of 70 trials: of 32 systems possessing all four success features, **30 (94%) improved clinical practice; systems lacking any of the four succeeded in only 18 of 39 cases (46%)** — and when clinicians had to seek the system out themselves, success was **0%** (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC555881/). Failure is overwhelmingly a feature-set, integration, and governance problem.

The most visible failure mode is **alert fatigue**, with drug-safety alert override rates measured at **49–96%** in the canonical 2006 review (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1447540/) and still **93.5%** in a 2025 AMIA study of 196,225 alerts (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12919540/). Crucially, the same literature shows overrides are *often justified* — with 36.5–39% of alerts judged false positives and reviewers agreeing with clinicians' override decisions in 95.6% of valid-alert overrides (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1447540/) — which reframes the problem: the engine's knowledge artifact is frequently the defect, not the clinician.

Beyond fatigue, three quieter failure modes are at least as dangerous: **silent malfunctions** (93% of surveyed CMIOs had experienced at least one CDS malfunction; failures-to-fire are nearly undetectable — https://www.researchgate.net/publication/299497160_Analysis_of_clinical_decision_support_system_malfunctions_A_case_series_and_survey), **model-performance collapse on real populations** (Epic Sepsis Model AUC 0.63 externally vs 0.76–0.83 vendor-reported; 67% of sepsis patients missed while alerting on 18% of hospitalizations — https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/2781307), and **governance/maintenance debt** ("In the absence of effective governance practices, implementation of CDS may fail, despite the purchase or development of a sophisticated system" — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3116253/). The integrating framework — Sittig & Singh's eight-dimensional sociotechnical model (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20959322/) — treats every failure as simultaneously locatable across hardware, content, interface, people, workflow, organization, regulation, and measurement.

## Key Findings

1. **Override rates of 49–96% are canonical**, and even "critical" drug-drug interaction alerts hit 88% override (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1447540/); a 2020–2025 literature confirms persistence: average override 46.2–96.2%, with appropriateness varying from 29.4% to 100% by alert class (https://www.sciencedirect.com/org/science/article/pii/S2291969420001970).
2. **The four Kawamoto features predict success with 94% vs 46% accuracy**: automatic provision in workflow, actionable recommendations (not just assessments), delivery at the point of decision, computer-based generation (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC555881/).
3. **Overrides are frequently rational**: two studies found 36.5% and 39% of alerts were false positives; adverse events followed only 2.3–6% of overrides (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1447540/).
4. **CDS malfunctions are common and mostly silent**: 93% of CMIOs had experienced at least one; two-thirds at least annually; current detection approaches are "inadequate" (https://www.researchgate.net/publication/299497160_Analysis_of_clinical_decision_support_system_malfunctions_A_case_series_and_survey).
5. **Vendor-validated ML performance does not survive contact with local populations**: Epic Sepsis Model AUC 0.63 (vs 0.76–0.83 internal), PPV 12%, missing 67% of septic patients while firing on 18% of all hospitalizations (https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/2781307).
6. **Even good models carry unavoidable alert burden**: TREWS across 227,091 encounters achieved AUROC 0.82–0.92 but PPV still only 0.13–0.26, with "high number needed to evaluate and high alert burden" (https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2781426).
7. **Automation bias is measurable**: clinicians follow erroneous CDS advice more often than no advice — risk ratio 1.26 (95% CI 1.11–1.44); 6–11% of "negative consultations" flip a correct answer to a wrong one (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3240751/).
8. **Alert burden is hyper-concentrated, not universal**: 88% of providers saw <1 alert/day while a few absorbed most of 196,225 alerts — and override rates *rise* with individual burden (98.6% at >5 alerts/day) (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12919540/).
9. **ECRI found that "for a majority of the PSO events we analyzed, the alert did not function as expected"** — a governance/measurement indictment more than a technology one (https://home.ecri.org/blogs/ecri-news/ecri-and-health-it-safety-experts-team-up-to-tackle-alert-fatigue).
10. **Go-live risk is real but era-dependent**: EHR-related patient-safety incidents were five times higher post-implementation (501 vs 82) in one university hospital (https://medinform.jmir.org/2021/8/e30470/); yet a 27-hospital pediatric study (2012–2022) found no overall HAC increase around modern EHR implementations (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10338103/).

## Detailed Analysis

### 1. Alert fatigue: the measured numbers

The foundational citation is van der Sijs et al. (JAMIA 2006), a review of 17 papers with quantitative data in 9: "Drug safety alerts are overridden by clinicians in 49% to 96% of cases" — except high-level overdose alerts, overridden at 27%. Per-study figures: Nightingale 2000 (90% overall; 85% high-level interaction; 93% low-level interaction); Payne 2002 (78% drug-drug/allergy; **88% of critical** interactions); Kalmeijer 2003 (90%); Weingart 2003 (94% overall, **96% medium-level**); Abookire 2000 (49–73% definite allergy alerts) (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1447540/). Their own observational work found "34% of the orders generated a drug safety alert of which 91% were overridden," including *all* alerts concerning admission medicines, at a rate of "one override per five prescriptions" (https://repub.eur.nl/pub/19379/).

The 2020s confirm the pattern rather than curing it. Poly et al.'s systematic review of 23 articles (2000–2019) found average overrides of **46.2%–96.2%**, with classified appropriateness varying **29.4%–100%** — worst classes were geriatric (14.3–57% appropriate), renal (27–87.5%), and drug-disease interactions; the top override reasons were "will monitor" and "patients have tolerated before" (https://www.sciencedirect.com/org/science/article/pii/S2291969420001970). The 2025 AMIA symposium study (196,225 dose/allergy/DDI alerts to 1,799 providers) measured an overall **93.5% override rate** — 98.9% for duplicate-allergy vs 87.7% for DDI alerts — and showed override rates *rising* with per-provider burden (98.6% among providers receiving >5 alerts/day). Equally important: 88% of providers saw <1 alert/day, so the burden is concentrated in a minority of clinicians absorbing most false-positive interruptions (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12919540/).

Two structural insights from the same review literature: first, sensitivity itself is software-dependent — a benchmark of 16 DDIs across commercial pharmacy systems found sensitivity ranging 0.44–0.88, "a gold standard … does not exist" (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1447540/). Second, overrides often reflect a broken artifact, not a careless clinician: reviewers agreed with clinicians in 95.6% of overrides of *valid* alerts, and 48% of even true-positive alerts were overridden; adverse events followed only 2.3–6% of overrides (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1447540/).

### 2. The determinants-of-success literature, inverted into a failure taxonomy

Kawamoto et al. (BMJ 2005; 70 controlled trials) is the canonical predictor study: CDS significantly improved practice in 68% of trials; meta-regression identified four independent predictors — **automatic provision as part of workflow** (P<0.00001), **actionable recommendations** rather than assessments (P=0.0187), **delivery at the time and location of decision-making** (P=0.0263), **computer-based generation** (P=0.0294). The headline result: "Of 32 systems possessing all four features, 30 (94%) succeeded; systems lacking any of them improved practice in only 18 of 39 cases (46%)" (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC555881/). Supporting experiments: 75% success when advice was provided automatically vs **0% when clinicians had to seek the system out** (rate difference 75%, 95% CI 37–84); requiring a reason to reject advice added 41 percentage points; recommendations over assessments added 35 points (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC555881/).

Read in inversion, this is the field's failure taxonomy — later literature keeps rediscovering it:

- Rely on clinician initiative → near-zero uptake (0% success in Kawamoto's data).
- Interrupt workflow without patient-specificity → rejection; van der Sijs's error-producing conditions — low specificity, low sensitivity, unclear information content, unnecessary workflow disruption — map exactly onto alert fatigue (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1447540/).
- Give assessments without actions → ignored. A British study cited by van der Sijs found 57% of prescribing errors came from *incorrectly executing an adequate plan* under busyness/interruption — the context matters more than knowledge deficits (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1447540/).
- Hard-stops backfire: they are "unacceptable… because decision support cannot replace the physicians' responsibility for the treatment of the patient" (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1447540/).

Kawamoto's unifying principle is the design sentence worth memorizing: "an effective clinical decision support system must minimise the effort required by clinicians to receive and act on system recommendations" (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC555881/).

### 3. Case studies and regulatory hazard listings

**Epic Sepsis Model (ESM).** Wong et al. (JAMA Internal Medicine 2021, Michigan Medicine; 27,697 patients, 38,455 hospitalizations): external AUC **0.63 (0.62–0.64)** vs Epic's internal documentation of 0.76–0.83; at the recommended threshold (score ≥6): sensitivity 33%, specificity 83%, **PPV 12%**; median lead time 2.5 hours; poor calibration at all horizons. Operational translation: ESM "did not identify 1709 patients with sepsis (67%) despite generating alerts… for 6971 of all 38 455 hospitalized patients (18%), thus creating a large burden of alert fatigue" — and it identified only 183/2552 (7%) of septic patients missed by clinicians, i.e., almost no incremental value beyond standard care. The authors show why time-horizon AUC framing (0.72–0.76, or 0.80 if scores ≤3h pre-onset are included) flatters broken models, and warn that "even a small number of bad predictions … can cause alert fatigue" while barely moving vendor-reported AUC (https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/2781307; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8218233/).

**Contrast: TREWS (Johns Hopkins).** A prospectively validated sepsis model across 4 health systems and 227,091 encounters reached AUROC 0.82–0.92 — but **PPV still 0.13–0.26**, with "high number needed to evaluate and high alert burden" and marked institutional variability (https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2781426). An ED-focused external validation similarly reported PPV 7.6%. Structural lesson: with a low-prevalence target, even a good model generates unavoidable alert burden without site-level re-calibration.

**Go-live harm history and its evolution.** The pediatric literature records Han et al.'s Pediatrics 2005 finding of an unexpected mortality increase after commercial CPOE installation at a children's hospital, against conflicting single-site replications; a 27-hospital Solutions for Patient Safety study (2012–2022) found **no overall increase in hospital-acquired-condition rates** around EHR implementations — contemporary implementations manage transition risk better than early ones, though the study covers inpatient HACs only (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10338103/). Meanwhile the measurement instrument matters: Palojoki et al. found total safety-event reporting rose 58.5% across an EHR implementation period, with **EHR-related incidents five times higher post-implementation (501 vs 82)** (https://medinform.jmir.org/2021/8/e30470/). Trade press ("Death by 1000 Clicks") documents the systemic layer (https://psnet.ahrq.gov/issue/death-1000-clicks-where-electronic-health-records-went-wrong).

**Regulatory hazard framing.** ECRI's Partnership for Health IT Patient Safety white paper set four safe practices for CPOE alert fatigue — governance, monitoring, analysis, optimization — and reported that "for a majority of the PSO events we analyzed, the alert did not function as expected" (https://home.ecri.org/blogs/ecri-news/ecri-and-health-it-safety-experts-team-up-to-tackle-alert-fatigue). ECRI's 2024 Top 10 Patient Safety Concerns lists "Unintended Consequences of Technology Adoption," citing AI dependence on training-data quality, bias toward populations resembling training data, scaling/integration difficulty, transparency gaps, liability uncertainty, and cognitive-overload-driven misuse (https://nursingthefuture.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/ECRI_Top_10_Patient_Safety_Concerns_2024.pdf). The 2024 Top 10 Health Technology Hazards highlights insufficient AI governance as a first-time topic (https://home.ecri.org/blogs/ecri-thought-leadership-resources/top-10-health-technology-hazards-for-2024-executive-brief).

### 4. Governance debt and silent malfunction: the failure mode nobody sees

**Silent malfunctions.** Wright et al. (JAMIA 2016; case series + CMIO survey, Brigham and Women's) documented four real malfunctions: (1) an amiodarone thyroid-monitoring alert died when another system changed amiodarone's internal identifier; (2) a child lead-screening alert broke after a rule edit; (3) wrongly-firing alerts; (4) an external drug-classification feed caused antiplatelet suggestions in patients already on aspirin. The survey: **93% of CMIOs had experienced ≥1 CDS malfunction; two-thirds at least annually**; failures-to-fire are especially hard to detect and "current approaches for preventing and detecting such malfunctions are inadequate" (https://www.researchgate.net/publication/299497160_Analysis_of_clinical_decision_support_system_malfunctions_A_case_series_and_survey). The asymmetry matters for any engine design: free-text bypass, stale allergy data, and missing lab integration degrade *sensitivity* invisibly (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1447540/).

**Governance debt.** Wright et al.'s five-site governance study is blunt: "Careful consideration of governance issues … can be as important as the quality of the decision support itself. In the absence of effective governance practices, implementation of CDS may fail, despite the purchase or development of a sophisticated system. A notable example … is the decision-support system at Cedars-Sinai Medical Center that had to be shut down because of usability issues." Content management — continuous tracking and updating of rules post-implementation — was a defining discipline, with recommended practices spanning CDS-specific committees and defined approval workflows (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3116253/).

**The operationalized response.** ECRI frames alert fatigue as a governance/measurement problem: monitor CDS metrics in near-real time, analyze impact vs burden, routinely optimize/tailor alerts (https://home.ecri.org/blogs/ecri-news/ecri-and-health-it-safety-experts-team-up-to-tackle-alert-fatigue). Vendor-side engineering confirms the pattern: off-the-shelf CDS tools "ship with generic rule sets and static thresholds that do not match local populations, formularies, or workflows," driving override rates up until value collapses (https://www.zymr.com/blog — trade press, low evidentiary weight but consistent with peer-reviewed sources).

### 5. Trust, automation bias, deskilling, and habituation

Goddard et al. (JAMIA 2012; 74 studies screened, 15 healthcare) found clinicians follow erroneous CDS advice *more* often than no advice: **risk ratio 1.26 (95% CI 1.11–1.44)** across four controlled experiments. "Negative consultations" — a correct unaided answer flipped to wrong after advice — occurred in 6–11% of cases in the underlying studies (e.g., Westbrook: answers improved 29%→50% correct, but 7% correct-before answers became wrong-after). Moderators: task inexperience increases bias risk (yet novices benefit most overall — the junior-staff trap); trust-vs-self-confidence calibration is the primary driver; time pressure pushes heuristic acceptance. Evidence-backed mitigations: displaying the DSS reasoning, per-recommendation confidence levels, reducing prominence of bad advice, accountability, and training (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3240751/).

**Habituation** completes the loop: field studies of 66,049 logged alerts across 1,152 physicians show dismissal becomes habitual "without significant cognitive considerations" — context stability plus repetition plus reinforcement (https://www.researchgate.net/publication/303832347_Alert_override_as_a_habitual_behavior_-_A_new_perspective_on_a_persistent_problem). On the workload direction: ECRI's 2024 guidance warns burnout and cognitive overload drive *over*-reliance on AI — the same overload that produces fatigue-driven dismissal elsewhere (https://nursingthefuture.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/ECRI_Top_10_Patient_Safety_Concerns_2024.pdf). Deskilling beyond trainee-exercise effects (noted in van der Sijs's cited renal-dosing work) remains less studied than commission errors — flagged in Goddard's own limitations (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3240751/).

### 6. Data quality, validation, and drift

Van der Sijs enumerates the upstream sensitivity killers that make well-written rules useless in practice: turned-off/bypassed alerting, incomplete or stale knowledge databases, uncoded/free-text allergies, missing weight data, absent serum-level logic, new drugs absent from the database, no lab-based triggering — requiring standardized, regularly updated drug databases as the systemic fix (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1447540/). Wright's malfunction cases show how *interfaces between systems* silently mutate semantics — identifier changes and external classification feeds killing alerts without any user noticing (https://www.researchgate.net/publication/299497160_Analysis_of_clinical_decision_support_system_malfunctions_A_case_series_and_survey).

Population drift and validation gaps are documented quantitatively in sepsis: vendor internal AUC 0.76–0.83 vs deployed 0.63, with many alerts firing a median 7 hours *after* first lactate measurement — i.e., sepsis already clinically apparent (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8218233/; https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/2781307). Even strong models degrade across sites (AUROC 0.82–0.92 spread, PPV 0.13–0.26; https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2781426). ECRI institutionalizes the response: pre-deployment *and ongoing* validation on local historical data, bias monitoring, and lifecycle maintenance protocols (https://nursingthefuture.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/ECRI_Top_10_Patient_Safety_Concerns_2024.pdf).

### 7. The integrating framework

Sittig & Singh's **eight-dimensional sociotechnical model** — (1) hardware/software infrastructure, (2) clinical content, (3) human-computer interface, (4) people, (5) workflow & communication, (6) internal organizational features, (7) external rules & regulations, (8) measurement & monitoring — treats CDS failure as locatable across interdependent layers simultaneously, not as a single bad component (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20959322/). Its application to health-IT-induced medication errors (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6402944/) treats every alert failure as multi-layer; van der Sijs made the same move a decade earlier with Reason's accident-causation model: active failures ← error-producing conditions ← latent organizational conditions (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1447540/).

## Contrarian Views And Risks

- **The strongest contrarian frame: CDS works when built right.** Kawamoto's four features produced 94% success in trials; failure is "overwhelmingly a feature-set/integration problem" (46% success without them) (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC555881/).
- **Overrides are often justified** — 36.5–39% of alerts false positives, ≤6% of overrides followed by adverse events — so the corrective priority is pruning false positives, not forcing compliance (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1447540/). Poly's appropriateness decomposition operationalizes this: duplication/formulary overrides are largely appropriate (prune those alerts), while geriatric/renal logic persists inappropriately (fix that logic) (https://www.sciencedirect.com/org/science/article/pii/S2291969420001970).
- **The 2025 AMIA data challenge the universal-victim narrative**: average 0.42 alerts/clinician/day, 88% of providers under 1/day — but the burden that exists is hyper-concentrated and accompanied by ~93.5% overrides, so the intervention fails precisely where it fires (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12919540/).
- **Perceived vs logged burden conflict**: subjective PCP reports of up to ~63 alerts/day vs objective logs under ~20/day — context collapse may be realer in some specialties than aggregates suggest (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12919540/).
- **Vendor numbers aren't necessarily dishonest**: metric selection (hospitalization-level vs time-horizon AUC; gold-standard definition) materially changes reported performance, per the Wong letters (https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/2781307).
- **Modern EHR go-lives are safer than early ones**: the 27-hospital null result on HACs tempers the Han-2005 mortality narrative — harms are localized and preventable with governance (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10338103/).
- **Turning alerts off wholesale is itself risky** if done "without careful error management" (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1447540/).

## Open Questions

- **"The" override rate does not exist**: 49–96% (van der Sijs) vs 46.2–96.2% (Poly) vs 93.5% (AMIA 2025, single center) — different denominators and alert types; any claim about override rates must specify both (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1447540/; https://www.sciencedirect.com/org/science/article/pii/S2291969420001970; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12919540/).
- **Appropriateness definitions conflict**: van der Sijs finds overrides produce ADEs in ≤~6% of cases; Poly finds up to ~70% inappropriate in some classes — expert-panel vs chart-abstraction definitions differ (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1447540/; https://www.sciencedirect.com/org/science/article/pii/S2291969420001970).
- **Reconciling implementation-harm history**: Han 2005 mortality increase vs SPS 27-hospital null (2023) vs Palojoki incident spikes — likely severity-mix, era, and measurement-instrument differences (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10338103/; https://medinform.jmir.org/2021/8/e30470/).
- **Gaps found**: no fetched source gives randomized evidence on alert-reduction interventions' downstream ADE effects; the ECRI 2023/2024 technology-hazards full lists were gated; the BMJ Quality & Safety effectiveness paper was paywalled and unused (https://qualitysafety.bmj.com/content/34/12/788); NHS-specific and BC-Children's EHR-transition reports did not surface under these search terms.
- **Deskilling remains under-studied** relative to automation-bias commission errors (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3240751/).
